If you've heard David Sinclair or Andrew Huberman mention apigenin, you're already ahead of most people. This modest plant compound — found in chamomile tea, parsley, celery, and citrus fruits — has quietly moved from obscure flavonoid to longevity-stack staple, and for good reason.

## What Is Apigenin?

Apigenin (4',5,7-trihydroxyflavone) is a plant-derived flavone that belongs to the flavonoid family of polyphenols. It's the primary bioactive compound responsible for chamomile tea's relaxing properties and is one of the most studied flavonoids in anti-aging research.

Unlike many supplements with a single mechanism, apigenin operates across several overlapping pathways — which helps explain why interest in it has grown considerably in recent years.

## Sleep and GABA Receptor Activity

Apigenin binds to GABA-A receptors in the brain — the same receptors targeted by benzodiazepines and alcohol. Unlike those compounds, apigenin binds with much lower affinity and without the same side effect profile, producing mild anxiolytic and sedative effects rather than sedation or dependency.

This mechanism is why chamomile tea has been used as a sleep aid for centuries. Clinical research confirms that chamomile extract (standardized for apigenin) reduces sleep latency and improves subjective sleep quality, particularly in older adults and those with generalized anxiety.

For supplemental use, doses of **50 mg apigenin** taken 30–60 minutes before bed have become a popular protocol among biohackers and sleep-focused practitioners.

## NAD+ Preservation and Longevity

This is where apigenin gets interesting from an anti-aging perspective. Apigenin inhibits CD38, an enzyme that degrades NAD+ — the critical coenzyme that declines with age and is central to mitochondrial function, DNA repair, and cellular energy.

NAD+ depletion is considered one of the primary drivers of the aging process. Strategies to maintain NAD+ levels (including NMN, NR, and now apigenin) are at the forefront of longevity research. While NMN and NR increase NAD+ precursors, apigenin reduces the degradation of existing NAD+ — making the two approaches complementary rather than redundant.

Studies in mice show that apigenin treatment increases tissue NAD+ levels, improves metabolic markers, and extends lifespan in some models. Human data is still emerging, but the mechanistic rationale is well-supported.

## Anti-Inflammatory and Antioxidant Effects

Apigenin inhibits NF-κB, a master regulator of the inflammatory response. Chronic low-grade inflammation (inflammaging) is strongly associated with nearly every age-related disease — cardiovascular disease, neurodegeneration, cancer, and metabolic dysfunction.

By modulating this pathway, apigenin may slow the cellular aging process that inflammation accelerates. It also scavenges reactive oxygen species (ROS) and upregulates the body's own antioxidant defenses.

## Brain Health and Neurogenesis

Apigenin promotes neuronal differentiation and stimulates the production of BDNF (brain-derived neurotrophic factor), a key protein for neuroplasticity and cognitive resilience. Research in cell and animal models suggests it protects against neurotoxicity and may reduce amyloid-beta accumulation associated with Alzheimer's disease.

## How to Use It

**Food sources:** Chamomile tea (the best dietary source), parsley, celery, artichokes, oranges
**Supplemental dose:** 50 mg/day is the most studied dose; some protocols use up to 100 mg
**Timing:** Before bed for sleep benefits; anytime for longevity and anti-inflammatory effects
**Stacking:** Works well with NMN/NR for NAD+ support, and with magnesium glycinate for sleep

Apigenin is well-tolerated with no known serious side effects at standard doses. As a plant compound, it's broadly safe for most adults.

## The Bottom Line

Apigenin sits at a rare intersection: it's a well-understood natural compound with mechanisms relevant to sleep, inflammation, NAD+ preservation, and brain health all at once. It's not a miracle molecule, but few supplements check this many boxes simultaneously. If you're building a longevity stack, it's an easy, low-risk addition with meaningful upside.
