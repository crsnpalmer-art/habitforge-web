Vitamin C is one of the most well-known nutrients in existence. Nearly everyone knows they need it. But the conversation has barely scratched the surface of what "vitamin C" actually means — and why the form you get matters as much as the amount.

## What Vitamin C Does

Vitamin C (ascorbic acid) is a water-soluble vitamin your body cannot produce on its own. You must get it from food or supplements daily. Its roles are wide-ranging and fundamental:

- **Antioxidant protection:** Vitamin C neutralizes free radicals, protecting cells from oxidative damage — a driver of aging and chronic disease
- **Collagen synthesis:** It's a required cofactor for the enzymes that stabilize collagen's triple-helix structure. Without adequate vitamin C, wound healing and connective tissue repair stall
- **Immune function:** It supports the production and function of white blood cells, particularly neutrophils and lymphocytes. During infection, immune cells rapidly consume vitamin C
- **Iron absorption:** Vitamin C converts non-heme iron (from plant sources) into a more absorbable form, making it especially valuable for plant-based eaters
- **Cardiovascular health:** Higher vitamin C status is associated with lower blood pressure, reduced LDL oxidation, and improved endothelial function

## Whole Food vs. Synthetic: What's the Difference?

When you eat a bell pepper, a kiwi, or an acerola cherry, you're not just getting ascorbic acid. You're getting a complex matrix of:

- **Bioflavonoids** (rutin, quercetin, hesperidin) — plant compounds that work synergistically with vitamin C
- **Enzymes** like tyrosinase that enhance absorption and utilization
- **Co-factors** that stabilize ascorbic acid and extend its functional life in the body

Synthetic vitamin C is isolated ascorbic acid — just the molecule, stripped of this supporting cast. Research suggests that whole food vitamin C is absorbed and retained more efficiently than synthetic ascorbic acid at equivalent doses. One reason: bioflavonoids slow the rate at which ascorbic acid is cleared from the body, extending its activity in tissues.

This doesn't mean synthetic vitamin C is useless — it absolutely works. But whole food forms deliver the same benefits with a smaller dose and a more complete nutritional package.

## Benefits You'll Actually Notice

- **Immune resilience:** Regular vitamin C supplementation reduces the duration of colds and may reduce the incidence in high-stress populations like athletes
- **Skin health:** Beyond collagen synthesis, vitamin C's antioxidant activity protects against UV-induced damage and supports a more even skin tone
- **Energy and stress response:** Vitamin C is highly concentrated in the adrenal glands and is depleted rapidly under psychological and physiological stress
- **Reduced oxidative damage:** Chronic oxidative stress is implicated in nearly every major disease. Adequate vitamin C is one of the body's primary defenses

## Dosing

- **RDA:** 65–90mg/day for adults — the minimum to prevent deficiency
- **Optimal intake:** Most functional medicine practitioners recommend 500–1,000mg/day for general health
- **Therapeutic range:** 1,000–2,000mg/day during illness or high-stress periods
- **Whole food advantage:** 250mg of whole food vitamin C is often considered functionally equivalent to higher doses of synthetic ascorbic acid due to enhanced bioavailability

Vitamin C is water-soluble and non-toxic at reasonable doses — your body excretes what it doesn't use. However, very high single doses (3g+) can cause loose stools in some people. Splitting doses throughout the day improves absorption and tolerability.

## Food Sources First

The best whole food sources of vitamin C include:
- Acerola cherries (~1,700mg per 100g — among the highest of any food)
- Bell peppers (~180mg per cup, raw)
- Kiwi (~93mg per fruit)
- Citrus fruits (~50–70mg per fruit)
- Broccoli and other cruciferous vegetables

## When to Supplement

If you're eating a varied diet rich in fresh produce, you may meet your basic needs through food. But supplementation makes sense if you:

- Experience chronic stress (which depletes vitamin C rapidly)
- Have a limited diet or low produce intake
- Are fighting an infection or recovering from illness
- Are an athlete with high oxidative stress from training
- Want skin, collagen, or immune support beyond baseline

Choosing a whole food vitamin C supplement — derived from sources like acerola cherry or camu camu — gives you the full spectrum that nature designed, not just the isolated molecule.

## What the Experts Say

*Opinions below are paraphrased from each expert's public work, interviews, and podcasts — not direct quotes.*

### Paul Saladino
Paul Saladino has engaged with the plant vs. animal vitamin C debate, noting that animal foods contain small amounts of vitamin C and that nose-to-tail eating (particularly liver and adrenal glands) provides meaningful vitamin C content. He questions whether carnivore dieters actually develop scurvy despite low vitamin C intake, suggesting context matters significantly.

### Dave Asprey
Dave Asprey has discussed vitamin C extensively, generally preferring whole-food sources or liposomal vitamin C for better bioavailability and the presence of bioflavonoid cofactors. He's a proponent of high-dose vitamin C protocols for immune support and antioxidant protection, and has discussed IV vitamin C in extreme health contexts.

### Dr. Raymond Peat
Dr. Raymond Peat has written about vitamin C primarily in the context of collagen synthesis, iron metabolism, and immune function. He views adequate vitamin C as important but achievable through diet — particularly orange juice, which he recommends regularly. His position is aligned with whole-food sources and moderate dietary intake rather than megadose supplementation.
