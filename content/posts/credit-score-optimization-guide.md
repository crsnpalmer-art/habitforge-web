A credit score is one of those numbers that quietly shapes your financial life in ways most people underestimate. The interest rate on your mortgage. Whether you qualify for an apartment. Sometimes even whether you get hired. Yet despite how much it matters, most people have only a vague understanding of how it's calculated — and an even vaguer idea of how to improve it.

This is a practical breakdown of how credit scoring actually works and the specific actions that move the needle.

## How Credit Scores Are Calculated

The dominant scoring model in the United States is FICO, used in the majority of lending decisions. Scores range from 300 to 850, and they're built from five weighted factors:

**Payment history (35%)** — The most heavily weighted factor, and the simplest: do you pay your bills on time? A single missed payment can drop a score by 50–100 points. Conversely, a long history of on-time payments is the strongest foundation you can build.

**Credit utilization (30%)** — This is the ratio of your current credit card balances to your total credit limits. A $2,000 balance on a $10,000 limit equals 20% utilization. The conventional guidance is to stay below 30%, but the highest scorers typically maintain utilization below 10%.

**Length of credit history (15%)** — How long your accounts have been open, including the age of your oldest account, newest account, and average age across all accounts. This is why closing old credit cards can unexpectedly hurt a score — it can reduce average account age and eliminate the history associated with that card.

**Credit mix (10%)** — A combination of revolving credit (credit cards) and installment loans (auto loans, student loans, mortgages) demonstrates that you can manage different types of credit. This factor is less impactful than the others and shouldn't drive major decisions.

**New inquiries (10%)** — Each time you apply for new credit, a "hard inquiry" appears on your report and can temporarily lower your score by a few points. Multiple applications in a short window raise more concern than a single inquiry.

## The Highest-Leverage Moves

**Pay on time, every time.** Set up autopay for at least the minimum payment on every account. A single 30-day late payment can stay on your report for seven years. This is non-negotiable.

**Attack your utilization ratio.** This is the fastest lever available. If you can pay down balances — or request a credit limit increase without adding to your balance — utilization drops and your score can improve within one billing cycle. The goal is under 10% on any individual card and in aggregate.

**Don't close old accounts.** Unless a card carries an annual fee that outweighs the benefit, keep older accounts open. Use them occasionally (even small purchases) to prevent the issuer from closing the account for inactivity.

**Become an authorized user.** If you have a family member or partner with a long, well-maintained credit account, being added as an authorized user can import their positive history onto your report — even if you never use the card.

**Space out credit applications.** Every hard inquiry has a small negative effect. If you're planning major purchases (a car or home), cluster necessary applications within a 14–45 day window. Most scoring models treat multiple inquiries for the same loan type within this window as a single event.

**Check your credit report for errors.** This is underutilized and high-impact. Errors on credit reports are more common than most people realize — incorrect balances, accounts that don't belong to you, or outdated negative items. You're entitled to a free report from each of the three major bureaus annually via AnnualCreditReport.com. Disputing errors that get corrected can produce immediate score improvements.

## Understanding the Score Tiers

- **800–850:** Exceptional. Best available rates on any loan product.
- **740–799:** Very Good. Near-best rates, qualifies for most credit products.
- **670–739:** Good. Qualifies for most loans; some rate premium applies.
- **580–669:** Fair. Higher rates, some products unavailable.
- **Below 580:** Poor. Significant barriers to credit access.

The jump from "Fair" to "Good" is meaningful. The jump from "Good" to "Very Good" is where you start accessing meaningfully lower interest rates. On a 30-year mortgage, the difference between a 680 and a 760 score can mean tens of thousands of dollars in total interest paid.

## Building Credit from Scratch

If you have little to no credit history, the fastest paths are:
- **Secured credit card** — You deposit funds as collateral, get a small limit, use it lightly, and pay in full each month. Most graduate to unsecured cards within 12–18 months.
- **Credit-builder loan** — Offered by credit unions and some online lenders, these products are specifically designed to establish payment history.

## The Long Game

The most important insight about credit scores is that they're fundamentally a reflection of financial behavior over time. There are no hacks that override years of missed payments or sustained high utilization. But the system is also responsive — meaningful behavior changes can produce meaningful score improvements within 3–6 months, and significant ones within a year.

Treat your credit score the way you treat your fitness: consistent, daily-level habits don't feel dramatic, but the compounding effect over years is substantial. Pay on time, keep utilization low, and let time do the rest.
