For years, arginine was the go-to supplement for boosting nitric oxide (NO) — the signaling molecule that dilates blood vessels, improves blood flow, and enhances exercise performance. There was just one problem: oral arginine is poorly absorbed and rapidly degraded by intestinal and liver enzymes before it reaches circulation. Enter citrulline — the amino acid that does what arginine promised, and does it better.

## What Is Citrulline?

L-citrulline is a non-essential amino acid found naturally in watermelon (the name comes from *Citrullus vulgaris*, the watermelon genus) and produced endogenously in the intestines. Unlike arginine, citrulline bypasses first-pass metabolism in the liver and gut, reaching the kidneys intact where it's efficiently converted to arginine — and then to nitric oxide.

The result: citrulline supplementation raises plasma arginine levels more effectively than supplemental arginine itself, and sustains those levels longer.

## The Nitric Oxide Connection

Nitric oxide is synthesized from arginine by the enzyme nitric oxide synthase (NOS). NO relaxes the smooth muscle lining blood vessels — a process called vasodilation — widening the vessel and allowing more blood (and therefore more oxygen and nutrients) to reach working muscles.

In exercise contexts, this translates to:
- Reduced oxygen cost during submaximal exercise (greater efficiency)
- Delayed onset of fatigue
- Enhanced muscle pump (the training-relevant blood volume increase)
- Faster recovery between sets through improved nutrient delivery and metabolite clearance

## Performance Evidence

The research on citrulline malate (citrulline bound to malic acid, which also supports the citric acid cycle for energy production) is particularly robust:

**2010 study, *Journal of Strength and Conditioning Research*:** 41 men performing a bench press protocol to failure took 8g citrulline malate or placebo. The citrulline group completed an average of 52.92% more reps and reported 40% less delayed onset muscle soreness (DOMS) 24–48 hours after training.

**2015 meta-analysis:** Across multiple studies, citrulline supplementation consistently improved high-intensity exercise performance with moderate effect sizes — particularly beneficial for repeated bout efforts (multiple sets, interval training) where metabolite accumulation limits performance.

**Endurance applications:** A study in cyclists found that 2.4g/day citrulline for 7 days improved time to exhaustion at VO2 max intensity and reduced perceived exertion at equivalent workloads.

## Cardiovascular Health Benefits

Beyond exercise performance, citrulline has meaningful cardiovascular applications. By sustaining higher plasma arginine and NO levels, it helps:

- **Reduce arterial stiffness:** Studies show citrulline supplementation improves arterial compliance, a marker of cardiovascular aging
- **Lower resting blood pressure:** Meta-analyses show modest but consistent reductions in systolic blood pressure (≈4–7 mmHg) in hypertensive individuals
- **Improve endothelial function:** The endothelium (blood vessel lining) relies on NO to regulate vessel tone; citrulline supports endothelial health, which declines with age and poor metabolic health

## Citrulline vs. Citrulline Malate

Two primary forms exist:

**L-citrulline:** Pure citrulline; doses of 3–6g are effective for NO production and cardiovascular benefits
**Citrulline malate (2:1 ratio):** Citrulline combined with malate (a citric acid cycle intermediate); the malate adds aerobic energy support and reduces ammonia accumulation during exercise; typically dosed at 6–8g pre-workout

For performance applications, citrulline malate is the more researched and commonly preferred form. For cardiovascular and general NO support, pure L-citrulline works well.

## Dosing Protocol

- **Pre-workout performance:** 6–8g citrulline malate (2:1), 45–60 minutes before training
- **Cardiovascular/general:** 3–5g L-citrulline daily
- **Timing:** Pre-workout for performance; any time of day for cardiovascular benefits
- **Stacking:** Pairs well with beta-alanine (complementary endurance mechanisms), creatine, and beetroot/nitrate for additive NO effects

Citrulline is well-tolerated with no significant adverse effects at standard doses. Unlike arginine, high doses of citrulline don't cause the GI distress that limits arginine supplementation.

## The Bottom Line

Citrulline is one of the most evidence-backed pre-workout compounds available — delivering meaningful improvements in training volume, endurance, recovery, and cardiovascular health through a well-understood mechanism. It's a foundation-level supplement for anyone training seriously or prioritizing long-term vascular health.
